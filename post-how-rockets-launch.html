How Does a Rocket Actually Launch?

I've been taking a rocket science course on Coursera from the University of Michigan, and it's been making me look at launches completely differently. Here's what I've learned — starting from the very beginning.

The Basic Idea

The secret behind every rocket launch comes down to one simple idea: Newton's Third Law — every action has an equal and opposite reaction. When hot gas blasts downward out of the engines at insane speeds, that downward push creates an equal upward push called thrust. For the rocket to actually lift off the launch pad, that thrust has to be greater than the rocket's own weight.

The Fuel

For a rocket engine to burn in space, it needs two things: fuel and an oxidizer. Since there's no oxygen in space, rockets have to bring their own supply of something to make the fuel actually combust. There are two main types. Solid fuel rockets mix the fuel and oxidizer together into a rubbery solid that burns from the inside out — once you light it, it's going and there's no stopping it. Liquid fuel rockets, like SpaceX's Falcon 9, use liquid hydrogen or kerosene combined with liquid oxygen. The liquid version is way more controllable — you can throttle it up and down almost like a car engine, which is a big deal when you're trying to land a booster back on a platform in the middle of the ocean.

Getting to Orbit

Here's the part that genuinely broke my brain when I first learned it. Just going "up" isn't enough to get to orbit. You also have to go sideways — really, really fast. Orbit basically means falling around Earth so fast that you keep missing it. You need to reach around 17,500 mph for that to work. Most rockets also ditch their lower stages mid-flight to shed weight — that's what you're watching when SpaceX lands their boosters back. Less weight means less fuel needed, which means you can actually make it.
