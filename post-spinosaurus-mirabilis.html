Scientists Just Found a New Spinosaurus — and It Has a Sword on Its Head

A new species of one of the most famous dinosaurs ever was just named in 2026, and it came from one of the last places you'd expect to find a fish-eating giant: the Sahara Desert.

What is Spinosaurus mirabilis?

In February 2026, scientists announced a brand new dinosaur discovery. This species is called Spinosaurus mirabilis. Now, I know what you might be thinking — "wait, isn't the scientific name for Spinosaurus already Spinosaurus aegyptiacus?" And yes, that's a real species. But S. mirabilis is actually an entirely different species within the Spinosaurus family, not just a new fossil of the one we already knew.

Scientists found its bones in the Sahara Desert in Niger, which might sound confusing at first — Spinosaurus are amphibious fish-eaters, so what are they doing in the middle of a desert? But here's the thing: 95 million years ago, the Sahara wasn't a desert at all. It was a thriving wetland full of rivers, lakes, and massive fish. The world looked completely different back then.

And unlike Spinosaurus aegyptiacus, which was found closer to ancient coastal environments, S. mirabilis was discovered deep inland along river systems. That actually tells us something important — it supports the idea that these dinosaurs hunted more like giant herons, stalking prey along riverbanks and shallow waters, rather than diving in like fully aquatic predators.

The Crest

Skull comparison of S. mirabilis (top) and S. aegyptiacus (bottom). © Keith Ladzinski / NHM

S. mirabilis had one of the most unique crest designs of any dinosaur we've found. It was massive, curved, and scimitar-shaped — and I keep thinking about what it would have looked like from above the water's surface. Maybe something like a shark fin? Just… much weirder.

Scientists think the crest would have been covered in colorful keratin — the same stuff that makes up your fingernails — which made it even more dramatic in real life than the bare bone suggests. It's thought to have been used to attract mates and intimidate rivals. Basically, the more impressive your headgear, the more seriously everyone took you.

When the team was uncovering and studying the fossils, lead researcher Paul Sereno compared the animal to a heron — specifically, a hell heron. Picture something wading slowly into the water on sturdy legs, completely calm, and then — gone. That's S. mirabilis hunting.

Why This Discovery is a Big Deal

Here's why this matters more than your average fossil find. The majority of Spinosaurus bones we had were absolutely destroyed in a WWII bombing raid — which means every new specimen is essentially irreplaceable. We're piecing together one of the biggest predators to ever walk the Earth from fragments, and that makes each discovery count way more than it would for, say, a T-Rex.

On top of that, every known specimen of S. mirabilis found so far was a juvenile — about 8 meters long. Which sounds massive until you realize that's not even fully grown. These were the kids. Which means somewhere out there in the fossil record, there are parents we haven't found yet. Pretty big parents.
